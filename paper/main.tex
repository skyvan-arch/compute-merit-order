\title{The Compute Merit Order: A Negative Result\\
\large Electricity cost is roughly one per cent of GPU rental revenue,
and the geographic curtailment sequence it implies is not binding}
\author{Compute Merit Order Project}
\date{Working paper --- draft, \today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A GPU cluster earns a compute price per GPU-hour and pays an electricity
cost per GPU-hour. The difference is a spread superficially analogous to
the spark spread in power markets, and it implies a shutdown price below
which a fleet is uneconomic to run. Because regional power costs differ,
shutdown prices differ, which is said to imply a global merit order in
which compute capacity curtails in a predictable geographic sequence. We
construct that merit order from open data for seven European bidding zones
and find that \textbf{it exists, binds in roughly two hundredths of one
per cent of hours, and is worth about a dollar per GPU per year}. Evaluated
at hourly resolution --- the resolution at which a curtailment decision is
actually taken --- the right to stop running is an hourly option on the
spread. Its intrinsic value is \SI{1.30}{\usd} per GPU-year in Belgium,
\SI{0.67}{\usd} in the Netherlands, \SI{0.12}{\usd} in Germany/Luxembourg
and exactly zero in the other four zones, and it is non-zero only at the
cheapest of the nine revenue bases we measure. Against roughly
\SI{7000}{\usd} per GPU-year of revenue that is about
\SI{0.02}{\percent}. We stress that an earlier version of this analysis
evaluated the same data against \emph{annual mean} prices and concluded
that no zone is ever uneconomic; that conclusion was an artefact of
aggregation, and Belgium's dearest hour exceeds its own break-even price by
\SI{56}{\percent}. Power ranges from \SI{1.0}{\percent} of revenue at H100
on-demand list prices to \SI{16.5}{\percent} at A100 spot, so the answer
depends far more on which revenue basis is chosen than on anything in the
cost model. Cross-zone dispersion in shutdown prices is \num{1.65}x on our
published sample, compressing to \num{1.19}x once non-power operating cost
is added --- an algebraic property, not a sample artefact. We recover
Azure's \num{1}, \num{3} and \num{5}-year committed rates from published
reservation data, a discount to list routinely described as confidential.
The spark-spread analogy survives only in a weak form: the mechanism is
real and priceable, but at current prices it is worth two basis points of
revenue, and the economics that matter for AI infrastructure lie in capital
recovery and siting rather than in dispatch.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Spark spreads --- the difference between the wholesale price of
electricity and the fuel cost of generating it --- are a standard tool in
power-market economics, used to decide when a gas-fired plant is worth
dispatching. An analogous quantity is often asserted to govern AI compute:
a GPU cluster sells compute at some price per GPU-hour, its main variable
cost is electricity, and when the compute price falls below that cost plus
non-power operating expense, a rational operator curtails. Because
industrial power costs vary substantially across regions, the shutdown
prices implied by this logic vary too, which would produce a global merit
order --- a predictable geographic sequence in which compute capacity
curtails as compute prices fall.

This paper tests that proposition and reports a negative result. The
mechanism is real and we compute it explicitly, but its magnitude is far
too small for it to bind at observed prices. The reason is a simple
quantitative asymmetry that the analogy obscures: for a combined-cycle gas
turbine, fuel is typically \SIrange{60}{90}{\percent} of short-run marginal
cost, so a change in gas prices moves the dispatch decision directly. For a
GPU fleet renting at on-demand list prices, electricity is of order
\SI{1}{\percent} of revenue, rising to \SI{17}{\percent} only at the
cheapest spot price we observe. A threefold difference in a one-per-cent
cost item is a two-percentage-point difference in margin, not a dispatch
signal.

We test two claims:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The compute merit order.} That regional power-cost
    differences produce a geographic curtailment sequence as compute
    prices fall (Section~\ref{sec:merit-order}). We test curtailment, not
    retirement: the two are distinct decisions with distinct cost bases,
    and conflating them is a category error we discuss in
    Section~\ref{sec:spread}.
  \item \textbf{Chips versus power.} That the binding constraint on AI
    buildout is grid interconnection and power availability rather than
    chip supply (Section~\ref{sec:constraints}).
\end{enumerate}

We are able to test the first and report it as not binding. We are
\emph{not} able to test the second with the data assembled so far, and we
say so rather than gesture at it (Section~\ref{sec:constraints}).

A note on timing. We do not claim, as earlier drafts of this project did,
that comparable compute-price data first appeared in 2026: at least one
commercial index has published weekly observations since July 2024
(\texttt{docs/SOURCES.md}), and our own sources file contradicted that
earlier claim. What is new here is not the existence of such data but the
construction of a \emph{freely redistributable} series from
vendor-published prices, which is what makes an independently auditable
spread computable at all.

\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Free-data constraint}

This project publishes its dataset under CC-BY-4.0 and holds itself to a
hard constraint: \textbf{a source is eligible only if it is free to access
and permits redistribution of derived values}. A number a reader cannot
fetch and re-derive is not auditable, and a number we are contractually
barred from republishing cannot enter \texttt{/data/final} at all. This
ruled out the two credible commercial compute-price indices
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) and, as described below, five of the twelve
power zones we collected.

\subsection{Power prices}

Day-ahead prices come from the Energy-Charts API operated by Fraunhofer ISE
\citep{energy-charts}, which requires no authentication. We collected 2026
year-to-date hourly prices for twelve European bidding zones. Energy-Charts
returns a licence \emph{per zone}, and they differ: seven zones arrive as
CC-BY-4.0 via Bundesnetzagentur/SMARD, while five exchange-sourced zones
(Spain, Portugal, Finland, Sweden SE3, Italy North) carry ``for private and
internal use only''. Those five are fetched and analysable locally but
their derived values are withheld from the published dataset; the omission
is recorded in \texttt{docs/WITHHELD\_ZONES.md} rather than left silent.
All results below therefore rest on seven zones, which is a materially
smaller sample than we set out to use.

Prices are converted from EUR/MWh to \si{\usd\per\kWh} at the ECB
monthly-average reference rate \citep{ecb-sdw}, mean \num{1.163365} over
the period.

\subsection{Compute prices}

Because no free redistributable compute-price index exists
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), we construct one from published cloud list
prices, which are facts about public offers and can be recorded with
attribution. We query the Azure Retail Prices API \citep{azure-prices},
which is free and unauthenticated, for a fixed basket of GPU SKUs across
three regions. Accelerator counts per SKU --- required to convert a price
per instance-hour into a price per GPU-hour --- are read from Microsoft's
public documentation repository \citep{azure-docs} and recorded per SKU
with their own source URL, never inferred from SKU naming.

We filter to Linux rows. Windows rows are excluded because they embed OS
licensing, which is not a cost of running an accelerator, and expired price
rows are excluded because they are history.

We publish five price bases: on-demand list, spot, and \num{1}, \num{3} and
\num{5}-year reserved. The reserved series matters. Committed-rate pricing
is widely described --- including in earlier drafts of this paper --- as
confidential and unobtainable. It is not: Azure publishes reservation
totals through the same free API, and dividing by the term's hours yields a
comparable hourly rate. The \num{5}-year H100 rate is
\SI{60}{\percent} below list. Any analysis using list price as the revenue
of a committed operator overstates it by roughly \num{2.5}x, and we
previously made exactly that error.

\subsection{Cost model}

Delivered power per accelerator-hour is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:delivered-kw}
  \text{delivered\_kW} = (\text{TDP} + \text{overhead}) \times \text{PUE}
  = (0.700 + 0.180) \times 1.25 = \SI{1.10}{\kilo\watt},
\end{equation}
held as a single named constant and swept over
\SIrange{0.9}{1.4}{\kilo\watt} in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}. We flag
that the \SI{0.180}{\kilo\watt} overhead term is a residual chosen to
reconcile the other two figures with a \SI{1.10}{\kilo\watt} total, not an
independent measurement.

\section{The compute spark spread}
\label{sec:spread}

Power cost per GPU-hour is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:power-cost}
  \text{power\_cost} = \text{delivered\_kW} \times \text{price}_{\text{USD/kWh}}.
\end{equation}

The shutdown price adds non-power marginal operating expense:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:shutdown-price}
  \text{shutdown\_price} = \text{power\_cost} + \text{opex}_{\text{marginal}}.
\end{equation}

Headroom against a compute price is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:headroom}
  \text{headroom} = \frac{\text{compute\_price} - \text{shutdown\_price}}
  {\text{compute\_price}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{What this cost is, and is not.} For a fleet whose accelerators
are already purchased, capital cost is sunk and therefore irrelevant to the
decision to keep running: the operator curtails when revenue falls below
\emph{avoidable} cost. Equation~\eqref{eq:shutdown-price} is that avoidable
cost, and it is the correct quantity for a shutdown question. It is
emphatically not the right quantity for an investment question. A fleet can
sit far above its shutdown price while being a value-destroying
investment, because capital recovery dominates its full cost. We compute
only the former, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} argues the latter is
where the economically interesting question actually lives.

We do not assign a point estimate to $\text{opex}_{\text{marginal}}$. No
public source for it was identified, so it is swept over
\SIrange{0.00}{0.20}{\usd} per GPU-hour and every result below is reported
across that sweep.

intuition behind the merit-order thesis is weaker than it appears.

\subsection{The order exists}

The curtailment sequence is well defined. At the midpoint opex assumption
and H100 prices, zones would go uneconomic in the order Austria, Norway
NO2, Germany/Luxembourg, Belgium, Netherlands, Sweden SE4, France. So the
qualitative claim --- that a geographic sequence exists and is computable
from public data --- survives.

\subsection{Resolution matters more than anything else in this model}

An earlier version of this analysis compared each zone's \emph{annual mean}
power price against the compute price, and concluded that no zone is ever
uneconomic. That conclusion was false, and the way it was false is the most
useful methodological result in this paper.

Curtailment is an hourly decision. An operator compares this settlement
period's avoidable cost against this period's revenue, and a spark spread
is evaluated per period for exactly that reason. Collapsing
\num{36281} zone-hours into seven annual means destroys the upper tail,
and the upper tail is the entire phenomenon. Belgium's mean power price is
\SI{0.114}{\usd\per\kWh}; its dearest hour is \SI{1.086}{\usd\per\kWh},
nearly ten times higher and \SI{56}{\percent} above its own break-even
threshold. Aggregation, not economics, produced the null.

\subsection{The right to curtail, priced}

Evaluated hourly, the right to stop running when the spread is negative is
a strip of hourly options on that spread, with strike equal to the compute
price. Its intrinsic value over the sample is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:option}
  V = \sum_{t} \max\!\left(0,\;
  \text{avoidable\_cost}_{t} - \text{compute\_price}\right),
\end{equation}
the loss avoided by curtailing rather than running regardless. This is
intrinsic value only --- no volatility model, no discounting --- and is
therefore a lower bound.

So the merit order is worth roughly \SI{1.30}{\usd} per GPU-year in
Belgium, and nothing at all in four of seven zones. Against on the order of
\SI{7000}{\usd} per GPU-year of revenue that is about two basis points.

This is a stronger statement than the null it replaces, in three ways.
It is positive rather than absent: the mechanism exists, binds, and has a
measurable price. It is a claim about a distribution rather than a mean,
so it does not evaporate under reaggregation. And it is robust to our
sample restrictions, because it is a tail statistic --- adding the
withheld high-price zones can only increase it, and adding cheap zones
such as ERCOT West contributes exactly zero.

\subsection{The ordering is not what the annual means suggested}

% CMO-CHECK: curtailment_option_value.csv | bzn=BE,benchmark_id=a100_spot,opex_assumption_usd_gpu_hour=0.05 | max_hourly_avoidable_cost | 1.2443
Ranking zones on the tail that governs curtailment, rather than on the
mean, reorders them substantially. Belgium moves from fifth to first.
Norway NO2 --- which on annual means looked anomalously expensive for a
nominally supply-rich zone, and which we previously reported as such ---
moves to \emph{last}: it has the lowest maximum hourly cost in the sample
and never goes uneconomic. On the statistic that matters, Norwegian hydro
behaves exactly as the supply-rich classification predicts. Our earlier
claim that the hub taxonomy fails was itself an artefact of the wrong
statistic, and we withdraw it.

\subsection{The dispersion is smaller than assumed}

% CMO-CHECK: merit_order_summary.csv | benchmark_id=h100_on_demand,opex_assumption_usd_gpu_hour=0.0 | shutdown_dispersion_ratio | 1.6451
% CMO-CHECK: merit_order_summary.csv | benchmark_id=h100_on_demand,opex_assumption_usd_gpu_hour=0.2 | shutdown_dispersion_ratio | 1.1948
The thesis assumes roughly \num{3}x variation. We measure \num{1.53}x at
zero opex, falling to \num{1.17}x at \SI{0.20}{\usd} per GPU-hour.

This compression is not an artefact of our sample; it is algebraic. If
power costs span $[p, 3p]$ and non-power opex is a common constant $c$,
the shutdown-price ratio is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dispersion}
  \frac{3p + c}{p + c},
\end{equation}
which equals \num{3} only when $c = 0$ and decreases monotonically in $c$.
The headline ``\num{3}x power costs imply \num{3}x shutdown prices''
inference is therefore false for any non-zero non-power operating cost,
independently of data. Since plausible non-power opex is of the same order
as --- or larger than --- the entire power cost, the dispersion that
actually drives a merit order is materially below the dispersion in
electricity prices that motivates it.

\subsection{Break-even power prices, and the limits of a mean-based test}

Inverting Equation~\eqref{eq:shutdown-price} gives the electricity price at
which a fleet sits exactly at its shutdown price:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:break-even}
  p^{*}_{\text{kWh}} =
  \frac{\text{compute\_price} - \text{opex}_{\text{marginal}}}
       {\text{delivered\_kW}}.
\end{equation}

% CMO-CHECK: break_even_power_price.csv | benchmark_id=h100_on_demand,opex_assumption_usd_gpu_hour=0.05 | break_even_power_usd_kwh | 12.8032
At the cheapest compute price we observe --- A100 spot, \SI{0.86}{\usd} per
GPU-hour --- the threshold is \SI{0.740}{\usd\per\kWh}. At H100 on-demand
list prices it is \SI{12.80}{\usd\per\kWh}.

It is tempting to compare these thresholds against zone \emph{mean} prices
--- the dearest zone in our sample averages \SI{0.129}{\usd\per\kWh},
roughly a fifth of the lowest threshold --- and conclude that the mechanism
can never activate anywhere. An earlier draft of this paper did exactly
that, and it was wrong.

Hourly prices breach these thresholds. Belgium's dearest hour reaches
\SI{1.086}{\usd\per\kWh}, \SI{47}{\percent} above the
\SI{0.740}{\usd\per\kWh} threshold; the Netherlands and Germany/Luxembourg
also breach it. What the break-even calculation correctly establishes is
therefore narrower than we first claimed: the mechanism cannot activate on
\emph{typical} prices in any zone we can foresee, so it will always be a
tail phenomenon rather than a persistent cost ranking. How much that tail
is worth is an empirical question, answered in
Section~\ref{sec:merit-order} at roughly one dollar per GPU-year.

The general lesson is that a threshold test evaluated against a mean tells
you nothing about a mechanism that operates in the tail. We record this
because it is the error we made.

\subsection{Why this cannot be rescued by better power data}

The gap is not a measurement problem. Two comparisons from our own data
make the scale clear. Azure's list price for the same H100 SKU differs by
\SI{3.69}{\usd} per GPU-hour between \texttt{eastus} and
\texttt{westeurope} --- a vendor pricing decision. The entire power-cost
difference across our seven zones is \SI{0.05}{\usd} per GPU-hour. The
revenue leg's regional dispersion is roughly \num{70}x the cost leg's, and
it is driven by commercial policy rather than by electricity.

Capital cost sharpens the same point. An 8-accelerator H100 node at
\SIrange{300}{400}{\kilo\usd} over four to five years is on the order of
\SIrange{0.86}{1.43}{\usd} per GPU-hour of depreciation before any cost of
capital --- roughly \numrange{6}{15}x the power cost. We do not publish
these figures in our dataset because we have no free, citable source for
node prices, and they are stated here only to size the comparison. But the
ordering is unambiguous: for any question other than short-run curtailment,
power is not the term that matters.

\section{Constraint analysis: chips versus power}
\label{sec:constraints}

\todopending{not tested. Interconnection-queue and grid-headroom data
collection has not been carried out, so this paper offers no evidence on
whether power or chip supply binds. We note only that the finding above is
consistent with power mattering for \emph{siting and capital planning}
rather than for dispatch, since a cost that is one per cent of revenue can
still be decisive when the alternative is not being able to energise a
site at all. That is a different mechanism from the merit order and
requires different data.}

\section{Sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

The conclusion is robust to every parameter we vary, because the result is
not marginal --- it is two orders of magnitude from the threshold.

\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrr@{}}
\toprule

\midrule

The honest way to state the fragility is this: the conclusion depends far
more on the \emph{level} of the compute price than on anything in the cost
model. If the relevant compute price is not the hyperscaler list price but
something closer to the spot price --- roughly a fifth of list --- headroom
falls from \SI{99}{\percent} to about \SI{95}{\percent}. That is still not
binding. Only a compute price near \SI{0.15}{\usd} per GPU-hour, some
\num{95}x below current list, would make the merit order operative.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

We list every material weakness rather than the flattering subset.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Seven zones, one continent, one partial year.} Licence
    restrictions removed five of twelve collected zones from the published
    sample, including Iberia and the Nordic zones that would most likely
    widen dispersion. Our \num{1.53}x is therefore a lower bound on global
    dispersion, and the US, Gulf, China and India hubs are absent entirely.
  \item \textbf{No capacity weighting.} A merit-order curve should have
    installed capacity on its x-axis. We have no per-zone GPU capacity
    data, so Figure~\ref{fig:merit-order} shows the cost ladder without
    capacity weights. The curtailment \emph{sequence} is unaffected; the
    quantity retiring at each step is unknown.
  \item \textbf{The compute-price leg is the weakest input.} No free
    redistributable index exists: Silicon Data's \texttt{SDH100RT} and
    GetDeploying's index are both paywalled, and the latter's terms
    prohibit redistributing raw data. We rejected both under the free-data
    constraint rather than cite figures a reader cannot verify, and built
    our own basket instead --- which is narrower than what we rejected.
  \item \textbf{One vendor, one segment.} The basket is Azure-only and
    hyperscaler-only. No neocloud provider is represented, so the segment
    dispersion we argue is central cannot yet be measured within a
    consistent methodology.
  \item \textbf{On-demand list price is not realised revenue.} Large
    operators transact on confidential multi-year reserved contracts at
    material discounts. This is the mirror of the PPA problem on the power
    side: both legs are public proxies for confidential transactions. Using
    list price \emph{overstates} revenue and therefore overstates headroom.
  \item \textbf{Wholesale power price is not delivered power cost.} We
    compare a hyperscaler retail compute price against a wholesale
    commodity power price. A real operator pays network charges, levies and
    taxes on top of the wholesale price, which in several European
    jurisdictions exceed the commodity component. This \emph{understates}
    power cost, possibly by more than a factor of two, and is the single
    most consequential methodological gap in this paper. Correcting it
    would raise the power share of revenue but would not plausibly close a
    hundredfold gap.
  \item \textbf{PUE and overhead are assumptions, not measurements.}
    Operator-reported PUE is self-reported and unaudited, and our
    \SI{0.180}{\kilo\watt} overhead is a reconciling residual.
  \item \textbf{Capital cost is excluded by construction.} This is correct
    for a curtailment question and wrong for every other question one might
    ask. Our own rough sizing puts depreciation at \numrange{6}{15}x the
    power cost.
  \item \textbf{All power cost is treated as avoidable.} That is wrong for
    any operator on an industrial tariff with a demand charge, or under a
    take-or-pay PPA, where a large part of the power bill is incurred
    whether or not the machine runs. Correctly splitting avoidable from
    unavoidable power cost would \emph{lower} the true shutdown price and
    make the merit order bind even less often than we report.
  \item \textbf{Claims 1 and 2 are in tension and we cannot resolve it.} If
    interconnection is genuinely the binding constraint, then grid capacity
    is contracted and largely pre-paid, and an operator holding a scarce
    interconnect runs it regardless of hourly energy price --- which would
    suppress the very curtailment mechanism Claim 1 describes. The two
    claims can only both hold under specific contracting regimes, and we
    have not established which regime prevails.
  \item \textbf{Azure spot is not an auction clearing price.} In our data
    spot sits at a near-constant \SI{18.5}{\percent} of list across two
    accelerator generations and two continents, which is the signature of a
    published discount schedule rather than a market. We use it as a low
    revenue bound, not as evidence about where capacity clears.
  \item \textbf{List prices are stale relative to power prices.} Several
    price rows carry effective dates years old, while the power series is
    hourly. Any spread between a sticky annual list price and an hourly
    spot power price is dominated by the staleness of the former.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The compute merit order is real, computable from open data, and
economically inert at current prices. Electricity is about one per cent of
H100 rental revenue; headroom over avoidable cost exceeds
\SI{97}{\percent} everywhere we can measure; no zone is uneconomic; and the
cross-zone dispersion in shutdown prices is \num{1.53}x rather than the
\num{3}x assumed, compressing further once non-power operating costs are
included.

The spark-spread analogy fails quantitatively. It works for gas generation
because fuel is most of marginal cost, so fuel-price differences drive
dispatch. It does not transfer to GPU compute, where power is a rounding
error against rental revenue. Regional power-price differences of the
magnitude observed cannot produce a curtailment sequence; they can only
produce small margin differences.

This does not make power irrelevant to AI infrastructure --- it relocates
where power matters. Three candidate mechanisms survive our result and each
requires different data than we assembled:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Capital recovery, not dispatch.} If accelerator
    depreciation dominates full cost, the economically interesting
    threshold is the price at which new capacity stops being \emph{built},
    not the price at which existing capacity stops running. Our shutdown
    price is deliberately the wrong tool for that question.
  \item \textbf{Availability, not price.} A one-per-cent cost item is still
    decisive when the alternative is being unable to energise a site.
    Interconnection queues and grid headroom plausibly bind on
    \emph{whether} capacity exists rather than on what it costs to run.
  \item \textbf{Compute price collapse.} If compute prices fell toward
    marginal cost, as they would in a commoditised market with excess
    capacity, the merit order would become operative. Our result quantifies
    how far that is: roughly a \num{40}- to \num{95}-fold decline.
  \end{enumerate}

We report this as a negative result because the data does not support the
thesis as framed. The project's next phase should redirect toward
constraint data and capital cost, where the evidence suggests the binding
economics actually lies.
